# Judge Q&A: the 16 toughest questions

Honest answers grounded in the code. File references are clickable. Numbers come from
[RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md) (generated by `python3 gridpact.py results`).

---

### 1. "How is this decentralized if there's a validator?"

The validator decides nothing about *who* gets power. It has no information to decide with: it
never sees urgency, damage, reserves or priority ([validator.py](gridpact/validator.py)). It runs
four physical checks: every agent present, whole watts, `0 ≤ a_i ≤ request_i`, and `Σa ≤ supply`.
Then it says yes or no. Think of it as the circuit breaker, not the dispatcher. The *split* comes
from the agents: each one computes its own bid from its own state and decides its own concession
each round ([agent.py](gridpact/agent.py) `concede_w`). Honest caveat: in this simulation all
agents run in one process and the "public table" is a shared Python dict. In a real deployment
the round would be a message exchange, but the information each agent uses would be exactly
what's in `PublicRound`.

### 2. "Why not just use an optimizer like linear programming?"

Three reasons. (1) **Information:** an LP needs one party that knows every utility's private
state (reserves, damage curves, dependencies). That central omniscient planner is what the
problem statement tells us to avoid, and it's a single point of failure in a disaster.
(2) **Objective:** the real objective is escalating and path-dependent (damage grows with
consecutive shortfall, and reserves carry over between steps), so a per-step LP isn't optimal
over the horizon anyway. (3) **Offline, zero-install:** an LP solver is a dependency. What we can
say honestly: an LP over the *full* 24-hour horizon with perfect information would be a useful
upper bound, and we haven't built that comparison. It's a natural next step.

### 3. "How do you prevent starvation?"

Three mechanisms, all in code:
- **Escalation:** each consecutive short step raises the agent's bid by `e` (8–10%), so a starved utility's urgency keeps climbing ([agent.py](gridpact/agent.py) `urgency`).
- **Fairness credits:** giving up more than the average share earns credits, and each credit adds +10% to future bids ([negotiation.py](gridpact/negotiation.py) `credit_ledger`).
- **Proportional sharing, not winner-takes-all:** every agent with slack concedes *some* of the excess, in proportion to 1/urgency, capped at 25% of its slack per round.

The evidence: longest single outage is 0.00 h for GridPact in every scenario across all 10
seeds, against 14.7 h (strict priority) and 2.8 h (proportional) in the cyclone.

### 4. "What happens if an agent lies about its urgency?"

We tested it rather than guess. RESULTS.md, "What if an agent lies?": a `greedy` agent inflates
its bid ×5 and never concedes. A lying Telecom cuts its own damage from 1,037 to 97, and
everyone else's damage rises 30%. **So lying pays, and we say so.** (A lying Hospital, oddly,
*lowered* total damage by 39%. That isn't a case for lying; it shows the negotiated split is not
optimal in that storm, and we list it as an open finding in RESULTS.md.) What the design guarantees:
(a) lying can never break physics, with 0 capacity violations while lying (the validator
doesn't trust bids); (b) bids are capped at 50, so the exaggeration is bounded; (c) credits are
earned only from *observable* concessions (offers are public), so you can't fake having
sacrificed. A real deployment would add metered verification (claimed reserve vs measured fuel
level) and penalties. That's future work, listed in RESULTS.md Limitations.

### 5. "How does it scale?"

RESULTS.md, Scalability: 4 agents ~0.15 ms/step, 24 agents ~1 ms, 96 agents ~8 ms, 192 agents
~26 ms (mean; timings vary a little with machine load). Rounds per contested step stay flat at about 3.4 (max 7) as agents grow, because the
round count depends on the increment rule, not on n. The cost per round is O(n²) in our
implementation, since each agent sums slack/u over the public table. That's why time grows roughly
quadratically. At 192 agents a 15-minute step still takes ~26 ms, more than 30,000× faster than
real time. Zero violations and zero fallbacks at every size.

### 6. "How do you *prove* there are zero capacity violations?"

Three independent layers. (1) Enforcement: integer watts and validator check C4, with a fallback
that is safe by construction and an all-zero last resort. (2) Tests:
[test_invariants.py](tests/test_invariants.py) runs every scenario × every policy × 4 seeds,
random live injections, a supply collapse to ~0, a deliberately broken policy that asks for
double, and 3,000 random fallback cases. (3) An independent re-check: the metrics module
recomputes `Σ alloc ≤ supply` from raw watts without trusting the validator, and a test
tampers with a record to show the re-check catches it. Plus the 160 runs in RESULTS.md: 0
violations.

### 7. "What does 'atomic' mean here, and how do you know it works?"

A step either fully happens or doesn't happen at all. All state objects are frozen, a step
builds new ones, and the only line that changes the simulation is `self.state = candidate`
([simulation.py](gridpact/simulation.py)). [test_atomicity.py](tests/test_atomicity.py) crashes
the step at each of 6 internal stages and checks that the state is *the same object* as before,
with no record leaked. It also crashes 10 steps mid-run, continues, and shows the final result
is byte-identical to a run that never crashed.

### 8. "Is it really deterministic? Show me."

`python3 gridpact.py run cyclone_landfall --seed 7` twice gives the same output.
[test_determinism.py](tests/test_determinism.py) compares full JSON step records (everything
except wall-clock timing) across runs, policies and scenarios. Randomness comes only from supply
noise, seeded per `(seed, step)`. Live event injections are logged with their step, and the
test shows a live session with button presses replays exactly. The "Results vs baselines" view
re-runs all four policies with the same injected events, so the comparison is apples to apples.

### 9. "Your damage numbers are made up, aren't they?"

The *model parameters* are hand-set (loads, reserves, weights in `data/`); we say so in
RESULTS.md Limitations. The *numbers* are not made up: every number on the dashboard and in
RESULTS.md comes from a real run, and RESULTS.md and the ALGORITHM.md worked example are written
by scripts. The claim we make is *relative*: on the same model, same seed and same events,
negotiation does 70–76% less damage than strict priority and 2–15% less than proportional. Change
the parameters in `data/` and re-run `python3 gridpact.py results` to check whether the ranking
holds.

### 10. "Proportional-to-demand is almost as good. Why bother?"

It's the strongest baseline, and RESULTS.md shows that openly (cascading substations: GridPact
only 2.3% less damage). What proportional can't do is *use reserves strategically*: it gives
everyone the same fraction whether their diesel tank is full or empty. In the cyclone that leads
to 1.7 failure events per run on average and 78 kWh of critical energy unserved, against 0 and 0
for GridPact. In a hospital, "0 failures" is the number that matters. Proportional also edges GridPact on
Jain's index (0.9993 vs 0.9992), and we report that too. You can watch this live: in the cyclone,
proportional is slightly ahead of GridPact from about 06:00 to 09:15 on seed 41 (GridPact is
spending reserves to keep everyone above critical load), then falls behind for good once its
utilities start failing.

### 11. "Why should the hospital ever give up power?"

Because sometimes it can afford to and the others can't. In the worked example
([ALGORITHM.md](ALGORITHM.md)) at 09:30, the Hospital still has 34% reserve and a 250 kW
generator, while Emergency Services is at 28% and holding firm. The Hospital concedes, runs its
critical load on diesel, and earns credits. When its reserve drops below 30%, it holds firm in
turn. Strict priority never lets the hospital give way, and that starves Telecom, which cascades
back into Emergency Services and the Hospital (Telecom → Emergency, Telecom → Hospital edges).
Strict priority ends up with 6–7 failure events.

### 12. "What if negotiation doesn't converge?"

Two cases, both handled and tested ([test_negotiation.py](tests/test_negotiation.py)).
**Deadlock:** survival floors exceed supply, so someone *must* fail. The deterministic fallback
gives survival floors in order of the agents' own bids (ties by id), then tops up. **Round
cap** (16): the same fallback, starting from the current offers. Both are constraint-safe by
construction. In practice the fallback ran 0 times across all 160 runs in RESULTS.md (max 7
rounds observed), but the test suite forces both paths.

### 13. "Isn't the 'negotiation' just a formula? Where's the agency?"

Each agent runs a decision policy on private information: how much reserve to spend (hold firm
below 30%), whether to discharge for comfort load (only above 50%), what to bid, how much to
concede given the public offers. Different private states produce different behaviour in the
same round. The `greedy` strategy shows the protocol works with heterogeneous agents. It is
rule-based, not learned. That's deliberate: every decision is explainable in a sentence, which
is what the log prints. A learning agent (for example RL on the bid) could slot into the same
`UtilityAgent` interface; we chose explainability for this round.

### 14. "How are cascades modelled?"

A configurable dependency graph in `data/agents/*.json`: Telecom → Emergency (35%),
Water → Hospital (25%), Telecom → Hospital (10%). If a source fails, the target's effective
service drops by `impact × its desired load`, which can push the target below its own critical
load. We iterate until nothing changes, so chains of any length work, and cycles terminate
(tested) because the failure set can only grow. Agents learn about cascades the way real
operators do: from public status, not from each other's internals.

### 15. "What would you need for a real city?"

Honest list: (1) real load and reserve data per utility, and damage weights set with domain
experts; (2) a network model (feeder limits, line losses), since today supply is one pooled
number; (3) verification of claims (metered reserves) and penalties, to make lying
unprofitable; (4) actual distributed messaging between agents, with timeouts; (5) a
perfect-information LP upper bound to measure how far from optimal we are. Steps 2–5 are
engineering on top of the current interfaces. The protocol, validator and atomic-commit design
stay the same.

### 16. "Does any of this work on a real city?"

We ran it on Mumbai ([CITY.md](CITY.md), and the "Mumbai, real data" page). The inputs are Census
2011 population for all 24 wards, published loads (hospital beds × measured kWh per bed, BMC's
50 MW of water pumping, tower count × TRAI's kW per tower), and recorded disasters: the 12 October
2020 grid failure, the 2005 deluge, Cyclone Tauktae, and a forecast of the 2020 failure striking
at the 2023 record peak. Every number links to its source, and every assumption is labelled.

The honest answer has two parts. First, real data found three bugs that our hand-set scenarios
could not: bids ignored harm per kW, concessions were absolute instead of proportional to size,
and the deadlock fallback could hand all power to an agent that would fail anyway. With the
original rules GridPact did 2–4× more harm than strict priority. All three are fixed (DECISIONS
D-17, D-18). Second, after the fix GridPact *ties* the best baseline on Mumbai, within ±0.4%, and
beats proportional sharing by up to 12%, because hospitals, water and telecom are under 3% of
the city's load. When critical loads are that small, protecting them first is near-optimal, and
the rest of the shortfall falls on homes, which no allocation can avoid. GridPact's 70–76%
advantage over strict priority appears when the critical utilities themselves compete for too
little power, as in the core scenarios. Zero capacity violations in 400 city runs.
